Things to Notice
Temperature

Notice that when the oxygen molecules start reacting with the carbon atoms, energy is released and the system heats up. As a result, free molecules move and rotate faster, and the carbon atoms remaining in the graphite vibrate more vigorously. Because of this higher temperature, the remaining oxygen molecules have a higher chance of reacting with the lattice, triggering a runaway effect until all the carbon is burned and converted into either CO₂ or CO.

Chemical Reaction

Notice that below about 800K, any reaction has a very low probability of happening. Between 800K and 1000K, an O₂ molecule colliding with the graphite will mostly react to form CO₂ directly. However, when the temperature is very high, collisions are more energetic and often produce a CO molecule and a free O atom. That free O atom can then either react with an existing CO molecule to form CO₂, or react with the carbon lattice to form another CO molecule.

Statistics

The simulation above is physically rigorous: the required activation energies, released reaction energies, and rigid-body collisions closely model real life. However, we are using a statistical shortcut. Because the simulation timescale is incredibly small (time steps of 10⁻¹⁴ seconds) to make the molecular motion observable, we only simulate a tiny fraction of a macroscopic system. In real life, trillions of O₂ molecules would hit the graphite every fraction of a second. To compensate for having far fewer molecules here, the per-collision reaction probabilities have been artificially scaled up. Otherwise, at this timescale, you would have to watch the screen for years to see a single reaction happen!
